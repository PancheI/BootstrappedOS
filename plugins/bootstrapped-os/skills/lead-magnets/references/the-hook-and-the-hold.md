# Give the hook, keep the answers

A lead magnet has two jobs that pull against each other. It must be useful enough on its own that the reader trusts the founder. And it must leave a real reason to take the paid next step. Give too little and it's a tease nobody finishes. Give everything and the paid offer has nothing left to sell.

The rule: **give the result and enough to act on; hold the case-by-case answers, the full method and the sources.**

## What you give
- **The result, with a number and its source.** Real numbers are proof nobody can fake, and they cost nothing to share.
- **Step one in full.** The reader does it and gets something. That's what earns the email or the reply.
- **One finding on their own case** (audits). The most expensive one, with the fix.
- **The shape of the whole.** The step names, the checklist headings, the list of what the full audit covers.

## What you hold
- **The answers for their specific case.** What their number means, which of the options is right for them.
- **The full rule set** behind the steps.
- **Your sources and where you found things.** The research is the work.
- **The rest of the findings** in an audit, listed by name with a lock, not with the answer.

## Name what you hold
Say it once, plainly: "The full audit has 6 more findings and the plan to fix them; members get it." A held part that isn't named reads as a trick. A named one reads as a fair offer.

## The line you never cross
- **Never hold what the title promised.** "The pricing checklist" means they get the whole checklist.
- **Never make it half-baked.** A teaser has to stand on its own as a hook.
- **Never gate after they've started.** Ask for the email before, or not at all.
- **Never charge for "free".** No card for a free thing.

## One noun, one number
The first screen carries one subject and one number. "Your checkout: 3 clicks." "Your free tier: 10x what a real user needs." A screen with five numbers reads as noise.

## How the author does it: the audit teaser, v1 vs v2
The founder sent free audits of a business's pricing and checkout as a lead magnet, one page per business, the full report held until they became a customer.
- **v1** packed several findings, several numbers and the method onto the page. His verdict: "confusing ... random numbers ... doesn't flow".
- He compared it with a one-insight report from another company. His verdict on that one: "I understand immediately, aha".
- **v2:** one noun and one number throughout, a handful of real proof points, one screenshot of the friction, one button. No source names, no method on the page. His rule from that day: "Give the hook, keep the answers for the call." And: "don't give away free info."
- An earlier rule from the same work: "make the teasers self-containing like a hook, not half-baked info."
- **Triage:** free audits were only done in full for businesses he could actually serve. The others got a short, honest note instead.

## How the author does it: one free playbook, the rest for members
His founder community put one playbook in front of everyone ("What to charge before anyone has paid") and moved the others behind membership. The first step is public; the rest opens after a free sign-up, so the email is the price. The newsletter opt-in is a separate checkbox, not the gate.
- An early playbook ran about 14 minutes. His verdict: "14 minutes, everyone would quit… it needs to be 5m."
- His rule for the content: "people love to read, resonate and process so they apply on their product, not conclusions for me."
- Reader path he approved: post -> blog post -> free playbook (email) -> newsletter -> membership.
